### Q2. Explain compound interest with a concrete example.
```
Compound interest occurs when you earn interest on both the initial principal amount and also on the accumulated interest from previous periods. This concept can significantly increase your investment over time due to exponential growth, as opposed to simple interest where only the original sum accrues at a steady rate each period based solethy alone of calculations without considering previously earned interests or dividends.

Here's how compound interest works using an example: 

Imagine you deposit $100 into a savings account that offers an annual interest rate of 5%, and the bank applies this interest to your balance annually (compounded once per year). Here’s what happens over three years without making any additional withdrawals or contributions.

- **Year 1:** At the end, you'll have earned $5 in interest ($100 * 5%), so now you owe a total of $105 at the close of year one (principal + interest).
  
- **Year 2:** Interest is calculated on this new balance. So your account will grow by another $5.25 in interest ($105 * 5%), making it now worth $110.25 when you check at the end of year two (principal + previous years' compound interest).
  
- **Year 3:** Interest for this period is calculated on your new balance, which means earning about $5.51 in additional income ($110.25 * 5%). Your total account value rises to approximately $115.76 after three years (principal + interest from previous periods).
  
Notice how the amount of compound interest increases each year even though your annual rate stays constant? This is because you're earning interest on an ever-larger base, which includes both your initial deposit and all previously earned interests. The effect becomes more pronounced over longer timeframes or with higher rates due to exponential growth – a phenomenon often referred to as "interest compounding."

This concept is fundamental in finance because it demonstrates how small differences can lead to significant outcomes when dealing with money, especially for long-term investments. Understanding and leveraging the power of compound interest could mean substantial financial growth over time – a critical aspect that every individual or institution should consider while planning their monetary strategies such as savings plans
```

### Q12. What is the difference between gross and net profit?
```
The primary distinction between gross profit and net profit lies in their scope of calculation:

1. **Gross Profit**: This represents a company's earnings after deducting only direct costs associated with producing goods or services sold by the business, such as raw materials cost (cost of goods sold - COGS). Gross profit doesn’t account for indirect expenses like overhead and administrative fees.

Here is how it works: 
Gross Profit = Revenue – Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)

2. **Net Profit**, also known as the bottom line or net income, takes into consideration all costs associated with running a business including COGS but extends further to include operating expenses like salaries and utilities, interest on loans if applicable, taxes paid by the company (corporate tax), depreciation of assets etc.

Here is how it works: 
Net Profit = Gross profit – Operating Expenses - Interest & Taxes + Depreciations/Amortization expense(s)

In essence, gross profit provides a snapshot of the efficiency with which a company uses its direct inputs to produce goods or services. Net profit gives an overall picture of how much money is left over after all costs have been accounted for and can be used by shareholders as dividends or reinvested back into business operations.

Here's another way: Gross Profit = Revenue - Cost directly involved in production, while Net Profit takes everything (including indirect expenses) into consideration to show what’s left after all costs have been deducted from total revenues and taxes are paid out too!
```
